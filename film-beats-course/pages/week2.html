Week 2

Chopping and the drum problem

Session A — chop

means slicing one continuous recording into pieces and putting each piece on its own pad, so you can replay them in a new order. It's the central move in sample-based music: you're not playing the original phrase, you're playing an instrument built out of the original phrase.

Step 1: Set markers

  1. Press the pad holding the sample you want to chop.
  2. SHIFT + [START/END] → marker setting screen verified.

Three ways to place markers:

  • Manual. Position with [CTRL 1], press [MARK] to drop a marker. Precise, slow.
  • . With the sample previewing, press the blinking pads 2–16 at the moments you want a split. Fast, musical, roughly accurate.
  • . Press [VALUE] → select AUTO MARK. Three modes verified:
    • TIME DIVISION — 2 to 16 equal slices. This is the one for orchestral score. Set 16 on a 4-bar loop and you get one slice per 1/16 note.
    • LEVEL — 1 to 10, places markers where audio exceeds a set level.
    • TRANSIENT — Hard / Mid / Soft. Good for percussive material, poor for strings and atmos.

Step 2: Assign to pads

  1. Press [VALUE] → select ASSIGN TO PAD → press [VALUE] verified.
  2. Pad colours: empty pads blink yellow, selected pads go green, occupied pads are dark orange.
  3. [CTRL 2] sets Gate on/off for the slices. Gate on is usually right for chops.
  4. [CTRL 3] arranges the split order.
  5. [MARK] executes.

Step 3: Play them out of order

That's the exercise. Take a 4-bar orchestral phrase, chop to 16, and build a new melody by playing slices in a sequence the composer never wrote. Record it as a pattern.

Step 4: Pitch and time

Press [PITCH/SPEED]. Two behaviours verified —

See the worked example in Part 0 for a real case of this decision: the same 146 BPM sample kept at full speed vs. dropped to its 73 BPM half-time, an octave down, under half-time drums. Sample both to separate pads before you commit to one.

  • VINYL mode ON — pitch and speed are locked together, like changing the speed of a record. Slower = lower and longer. Tape-like, and the sound most associated with this genre.
  • VINYL mode OFF (VARI mode) — pitch and speed are independent. You can drop a sample an octave without it getting longer.

Toggle with SHIFT + [VALUE] knob on that screen.

From firmware v2.00 there are VariPhrase-based time-stretch algorithms, selected with SHIFT + [VALUE] in the pitch/speed menu verified — :

  • Backing — for sharp attacks. Drums, foley hits.
  • Ensemble — for sustained material. Bass, pads, strings, drones.

Choosing the wrong one is the usual cause of stretched samples sounding smeared.

Tempo detection: with [PITCH/SPEED] open, set and the SP analyses the sample and sets its BPM. If it guesses wrong — common with orchestral material — switch to MANU and enter it yourself verified.

Session B — drums from non-drums

Films almost never give you isolated drums. You build them.

Step 1: Source the raw material

  • Kick — a low door thud, a heavy footstep, a distant explosion. Anything with weight below 150 Hz.
  • Snare — a slam, a gunshot, a slap. Needs a sharp crack.
  • Hat — tape hiss, film grain noise, a fabric rustle, a cymbal edge.

Step 2: Shape each one

  1. Trim hard with [START/END]. A kick is 100–300 ms. A hat is 30–80 ms. Cut everything after the useful part.
  2. Pitch down for weight: [PITCH/SPEED], VINYL mode on, drop several semitones. A door thud at −7 becomes a kick.
  3. Set the : SHIFT + [PITCH/SPEED] opens attack / hold / release verified. Short release on hats, longer on kicks.

Step 3: Layer and resample

Two sources per drum. The classic combination is weight from one and attack from another — a low thud for body, a sharp click for the transient.

  1. Put both on separate pads.
  2. Press both simultaneously and check they sound like one hit, not two.
  3. Press [RESAMPLE], record the pair as one sample, assign to a single pad in the 1–4 range.

You now have a drum kit that exists nowhere in the source film.
