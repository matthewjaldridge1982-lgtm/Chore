Before Week 1

Part 0 — The vocabulary you need first

Every procedure in this course is from the Roland SP-404MK2 reference manual (v2–v5) or the Moog Grandmother manual. Firmware caveat: menu paths shifted slightly between firmware versions. Check yours (SHIFT + pad 13 → UTILITY → SYSTEM) and keep the shortcut PDF on your phone — see Failure modes & sources.

Confidence tags appear throughout this course where instructions aren't certain: verified, moderate, unverified — check your unit.

Beats, bars, and BPM

A is the pulse you'd tap your foot to. (beats per minute) is how fast those pulses come. 60 BPM = one beat per second. Most of the boom-bap / Public Service Broadcasting territory this course lives in sits between 80 and 95 BPM.

A (Roland calls it a measure — same thing) is a group of beats, almost always 4. You count 1-2-3-4, and every time you land back on 1 a new bar has started.

4 bars = counting 1-2-3-4 four times = 16 beats. It's the default loop length in sample-based music because most film score and most pop phrases resolve on a 4-bar cycle.

This matters immediately: skip-back memory holds 25 seconds by default, so at any tempo above ~40 BPM a 4-bar phrase fits comfortably. It also tells you when a sample is "wrong" — if you grabbed 9 seconds at 90 BPM you have roughly 3.4 bars, which is why it won't loop.

Worked example: a real sample at 146 BPM

A tapped-along guess of 75 BPM turned out to be the half-time pulse, tapped about 3% fast — the sample measured out to 146 BPM, and half of 146 is 73, not 75. This is the single most common way a tempo guess goes wrong: your foot finds the slower, half-time pulse instead of the actual beat, especially on cartoon and library cues with a bouncy feel. If a tapped tempo and a measured tempo disagree by roughly 2×, check whether one of them is just the other's half-time pulse before assuming either is wrong.

Use 6.58 s as the trim target for a 4-bar loop at this tempo. If a chop lands near 6.5 s or 6.7 s instead, that's a error, not a tempo error — don't go back and second-guess the BPM for it.

Two usable framings of the same audio — worth deciding deliberately rather than by accident:

  • Keep the pattern at 146 and let it be a fast, driving cut.
  • Set the SP to 73 and program half-time drums underneath the sample playing at its native speed. This is the more common approach with cartoon and library material — the source keeps its energy while the kit sits heavy under it. Same audio, a completely different piece.

Half-speed playback ( at 73) drops the sample an octave too, which can turn bright brass into something much darker. Worth sampling both the full-speed and half-speed versions to separate pads before you commit to one — see Week 2, Step 4.

Playback behaviour

  • — the sample plays only while your finger is on the pad. Toggled globally by the [GATE] button. Use for chops and stabs.
  • — one press plays the sample all the way through; further presses are ignored until it finishes. Use for drum hits and dialogue lines you don't want retriggering.
  • — repeats until stopped. Use for drones and 4-bar beds.

Sample vs resample

— sampling records audio coming into the SP; resampling records the SP's own output back into itself. Resampling is destructive in the sense that you can't unpick it afterwards.

Transient

The sharp attack at the front of a sound — the stick hitting the drum, the door slamming. is how the SP finds chop points automatically: it looks for sudden level jumps. Film atmos and orchestral swells have weak transients, so transient-based auto-chop works poorly on them. Level-based or equal-division chopping works better.

Quantise

snaps your played timing to a grid (nearest 1/16 note, etc.). Full quantise makes things machine-tight and lifeless. This course deliberately asks you to leave one part unquantised.

Gain staging

means setting levels once, at the input, before recording. A sample recorded too quietly can't be fixed by turning it up later — you amplify the noise floor with it. Recorded too loud, it clips permanently. Aim for peaks around three-quarters of the meter, never touching the top.
